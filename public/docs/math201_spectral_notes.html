Vector Spaces and the Spectral Theorem

Revision notes with a worked symmetric-matrix example

1. Vector spaces and subspaces

A vector space is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication and satisfies the usual associativity, identity, inverse, and distributive axioms. A nonempty subset is a subspace when it is closed under linear combinations: for all vectors u, v in the subset and scalars a, b, the vector au + bv remains in the subset.

2. Eigenvalues and diagonalization

A nonzero vector v is an eigenvector of matrix A with eigenvalue λ when Av = λv. Eigenvalues satisfy det(A − λI) = 0. A matrix is diagonalizable when it has a basis of eigenvectors, so A = PDP⁻¹, with eigenvectors in P and their eigenvalues on the diagonal of D.

3. Symmetric matrices

For a real symmetric matrix, eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. The spectral theorem gives an orthogonal matrix Q such that A = QDQᵀ. Normalize each eigenvector to unit length to construct Q.

4. Worked example

A = [ 2  1 ]
    [ 1  2 ]

det(A − λI) = (2 − λ)² − 1
             = (1 − λ)(3 − λ)
Eigenvalues: 1 and 3

For λ = 3: eigenvector proportional to (1, 1)
For λ = 1: eigenvector proportional to (1, −1)
Normalized vectors: q₁ = (1,1)/√2, q₂ = (1,−1)/√2

Thus Q has columns q₁, q₂ and D = diag(3, 1). Check the result by multiplying QDQᵀ and confirming that it recovers A.

Common checks

  • Verify each eigenvector by direct substitution into Av = λv.
  • For a symmetric matrix, check orthogonality before normalizing.
  • Keep the eigenvalue order in D consistent with the column order in Q.
  • Use rank and nullity to check dimensions of subspaces and eigenspaces.
